  1. A longer night of sleep 91 pts

    Moving the first bell later gives teenagers a better chance of getting enough sleep before a required school day.

    Increased sleep is central to choosing a later bell over retaining an early start. Objective measurements support meaningful gains, although a later start does not guarantee sufficient sleep. The current start time is unspecified, so the size of the proposed change is unknown.

  2. Support for adolescent sleep timing 91 pts

    A later start better accommodates the tendency for adolescents to fall asleep later as they mature.

    Adolescent circadian timing supplies a strong biological reason to favor later starts over retaining an early schedule. It also explains why simply demanding earlier bedtimes may fail. Biology supports the direction of change, while 8:30 is a policy recommendation rather than a universal biological cutoff.

  6. Better attention in first period 80 pts

    A later first lesson may help students concentrate on new material when they would otherwise still be drowsy.

    Attention directly affects the educational value of the first lesson and favors delaying an early bell. Experimental evidence supports improved attention, but does not establish the precise benefit for high-school first-period learning.

  7. Safer morning driving 79 pts

    Teen drivers given more opportunity to sleep may be more alert on the morning trip to school.

    Avoiding crashes has potentially exceptional consequences and strengthens the case against very early starts. However, the available study concerns overall adolescent crashes and a delay to 8:10, not specifically morning alertness or the 8:30 threshold.

    School start time change and motor vehicle crashes in adolescent drivers

  8. Neighboring districts control many events 75 pts

    External competition schedules create a genuine limit on unilateral changes and can increase missed afternoon instruction. This favors coordination or mitigation, and sometimes keeping the current schedule, but does not show that adopting 8:30 is infeasible.

  19. Other sleep interventions may cost less 59 pts

    A district should compare the effects and costs of a later start with sleep education and homework scheduling changes.

    Comparing costs and effects is sensible, but lower spending is not equivalent to better cost-effectiveness. The argument does not demonstrate that education or homework changes can replace later starts, so it supports evaluation more strongly than keeping the current bell.

    Breakfast access is a plausible secondary benefit, but additional morning time may instead become additional sleep. School breakfast provision could address the problem without changing the bell, so this argument only weakly distinguishes the two positions.
